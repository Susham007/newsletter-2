Dissent & Delimitation

One number decides how loud each Indian gets to be in Parliament — and in April 2026, the attempt to change that number failed on the floor of the Lok Sabha by 54 votes. This edition reads delimitation as a fight over representation itself: who gains a voice, who loses one, and why the arithmetic was rejected. The dissent it provoked runs from the southern statehouses to the street.

Delimitation is usually argued in the abstract — "the south loses, the north gains." Here you can do it yourself. Set how big the Lok Sabha should be, set how fiercely to protect the seats states already hold, and the map of India redraws in front of you, every state shaded by what the rule would cost or grant it. The politics stops being a slogan and becomes a dial.

The rule underneath is the same one the Constitution reaches for: seats in proportion to population. At the extreme — pure population, no protection — a seat means the same number of people everywhere, and states that grew fastest gain most while states that stabilised their population lose share. That is not a bug the reformers introduced; it is what "one person, one vote, one value" produces when some populations tripled and others held steady. The protection lever is the whole political argument in one slider: how much do we soften that pure logic to keep faith with the states that did what the nation asked and slowed their growth?

The map shades every state by how its seat share changes under the current rule — the true measure of representation, since a state can gain seats yet still lose voice if others gain more.

Watch the south as you drag. At today's 543 seats with full protection, nothing moves — that is the status quo, and it is why the freeze has held since 1976: standing still is the only outcome that hurts no one. Now switch to pure population. Tamil Nadu, Kerala and the others do not merely fail to gain; several lose seats outright, because a House of 543 redistributed by 2011 population takes from the states that grew slowly to give to those that grew fast. This is the exact projection that drove the southern revolt: at a constant House, Tamil Nadu falls from 39 seats to 32 and Kerala from 20 to 15.

A seat is not a reward for growing. But under pure population, holding your population steady is punished exactly as if it were a failure.

The reformers' answer is the "balanced additions" preset: expand the House to 848, keep every existing seat, and hand out only the new seats by population. Now no state loses a seat in absolute terms — but watch the share column, because share is what decides who can block a bill and who can pass one. Even when Tamil Nadu keeps all 39 seats, its share of a bigger House still falls, because the north's additions are larger. This is the trap at the heart of the debate: there is no expansion large enough to hold every state's share constant while also making seats proportional to population. Protecting seats and protecting share are different promises, and the arithmetic will not let you keep both.

That is the lab's real lesson, and it is uncomfortable for every side. The status quo is unfair to the populous north, whose voters are under-represented today. Pure population is a shock to the south, which is asked to lose voice for having succeeded at population policy. And the middle paths — protect seats, expand the House — soften the blow to seats while still shifting the balance of power northward, because power lives in share, not in seat count. Drag the levers to any honest setting and the same shadow appears: someone's relative voice must fall for someone else's to rise. Delimitation is not a technical readjustment. It is a transfer of power with a formula for a face.

Passage Probability

Source: Lok Sabha vote record, 17 April 2026 · Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 · PRS Legislative Research · Article 368

On 17 April 2026, the government tried to expand the Lok Sabha to 850 seats and unfreeze delimitation. It had a clear majority in the room. It lost anyway — because expanding representation is one of the few things a simple majority cannot do. This is the arithmetic of why, made playable.

Most bills need more people to say yes than no. A constitutional amendment needs something much harder: under Article 368, two-thirds of the members present and voting in each House, and — because delimitation changes the representation of states — ratification by the legislatures of at least half the states. The government of the day commands a majority. It does not command two-thirds. That single gap is the story of 17 April, and it is the reason the freeze that everyone calls unfair has survived every government that called it unfair.

2 · Or empty the benches
Article 368 counts two-thirds of members present and voting — so every opposition absence lowers the bar without adding a single vote to the government's column.
The finding most coverage missed Toggle every swingable bloc and the government is still short in both Houses — because on delimitation the biggest opposition blocs are the southern and regional parties that lose seat share, and no amount of persuasion moves a party against its own survival. Now leave the blocs alone and drag the absence sliders. The bar falls without a single vote changing sides, because two-thirds counts only those present and voting. An opposition MP who stays home helps the bill pass exactly as much as one who crosses the floor — and can still tell their state they never voted for it. On 17 April the opposition understood this precisely: it did the opposite, packing the benches to raise the denominator. Defection is the story everyone writes. Attendance is the one that decided it.
A government can win the room and still lose the vote. On delimitation, the votes it needs belong to the states it would take the votes from.

The seven gates

Even a bill that clears both Houses is not law, and even a law is not a redrawn map. A delimitation package has to pass through seven gates in sequence, and in April 2026 it died at the second. Here is the full route, with this bill's actual fate marked at each stage.

The pattern the gates reveal is that India's founders built delimitation to be hard on purpose. A change this fundamental — reweighting every citizen's share of national power — was placed behind the highest bar the Constitution has, and then behind the states as well. The freeze everyone calls a distortion is not an accident of neglect; it is the predictable resting state of a system that requires near-consensus to move and offers none of the parties enough reason to supply it. The north cannot get the two-thirds without the south, and the south will not give the two-thirds for a formula that costs it share. So the arithmetic sits, frozen, until someone finds a number that lets both sides keep a promise — and, as the lab next door shows, that number may not exist.

Which is the quiet finding under all of it. Passage probability today is not low because the politics is heated. It is low because the mathematics is closed: the same proportional logic that makes reform just is what makes it unpassable, because it cannot deliver a majority its two-thirds without taking from the very states whose ratification it also needs. The 54-vote gap on 17 April was not a failure of whipping. It was the structure, working as designed.
