Revolution's Sound of Music

A view from the editorial board · protest music across Bangladesh, Nepal & India · opinion, offered as opinion
“১২ মাসে ১২ ফুল রে,
ফুইট্টা থাকে ডালে রে…”

Twelve months, twelve flowers, blooming on the branches. Evening falls, the nagor arrives, golden, moon-like, beautiful. The flowers bloom, their fragrance fills the heart. Tumi amar koto je apon — you are so dear to me. And then, almost petulantly, almost tenderly, comes the refrain: "Dekha na dile, bondhu, kotha koiyo na."

If you don't show up, friend, don't speak.

There is, objectively, very little here that sounds like revolution.

There is a lover. There are flowers. There is a bee wandering between gardens. There is dusk, longing, departure, the rather philosophical realisation that prem-i jibon, prem-i moron — love is life, love is death. It is tender, almost painfully so. And yet, Kotha Koiyo Na, the 2023 Coke Studio Bangla rendition of the Maimansingha Gitika, found itself sitting rather comfortably in the cultural universe of one of South Asia's most politically charged moments. A year later, Bangladesh would have its own rather brilliant response: Kotha Ko — speak up. The former says don't speak; the latter demands that you do. Perhaps this is where we ought to begin thinking about Gen-Z and protest music across Bangladesh, Nepal and India.

Not with the placard. Not with the barricade. Not even with Instagram. With the song.

Because dissent requires art, culture and space, and in all of that, perhaps the most unifying aspect is sound. A protest needs a street, but a street can be emptied. It needs a placard, but a placard can be torn down. It needs a speaker, but a speaker can be arrested, censored or drowned out. Sound is more difficult to contain. Perhaps that is why, irrespective of your political leanings, when it comes to dissent, Azaadi has a rather inconvenient habit of ringing true — and loud.

Hum kya chahte?
Azaadi.

It does not really matter whether you first heard it in Kashmir, at a university protest, in Gully Boy, at Shaheen Bagh, or through some grainy Instagram video. The words have travelled far beyond the geography that produced them. They have acquired new meanings, new bodies, new voices. A Kashmiri political vocabulary became a student protest anthem; a slogan became a song; a song became a reel; the reel became memory. This is what sound does to dissent.

It travels.

And Gen-Z, across South Asia, seems to have understood this rather instinctively.

The tendency to understand protest through its most visible political forms — the march, the slogan, the speech, the occupation — often makes us forget that protest is also performance. Someone has to occupy the space. Someone has to speak. Someone has to sing. Someone has to decide that the street, the classroom, the university gate, the public square or the internet is theirs too. The question of who performs therefore becomes political the moment the sphere becomes public.

This was one of the things that made Shaheen Bagh so significant. The protest was not merely against the CAA and NRC; it became a public performance of women, particularly Muslim women, claiming space, speech, night and political agency. Art, poetry, food, speeches, friendship and the sheer act of sitting there transformed the protest site into something much larger than a road blockade. The politics was therefore not confined to the demand. The politics was also in the manner of making the demand.

And perhaps music does the same thing.

A song does not simply tell you what the protesters believe. It tells you how they feel together.

That distinction matters.

A speech has a speaker and an audience. A song has a chorus.

And once the chorus begins, the distinction between performer and audience starts to collapse.

Bangladesh The most delicious example

They perhaps give us the most delicious example. There is Kotha Koiyo Na, the lover asking another lover not to speak if they do not show up, and then there is Shezan's Kotha Ko, released during the 2024 student protests, which effectively takes that sentiment and turns it inside out: speak up.

The contrast could not be more poetic. One is a love song about waiting. The other is a protest rap about refusing to wait. One asks for the absent lover. The other demands an answer from those in power. Kotha Ko became one of the songs associated with Bangladesh's student protests, alongside tracks such as Awaaz Utha, Bangla Maa, Osustho Bangladesh and Rokto Gorom Matha Thandaa. It circulated rapidly online despite internet restrictions, with Kotha Ko reportedly reaching almost three million YouTube views within three weeks.

And this is where the politics of the song becomes much more interesting than simply calling it an "anthem". Because what exactly is an anthem? It is not merely a popular song. It is a song that has found a collective body. A line that thousands of people know. A rhythm that allows thousands of individual frustrations to become one sound.

The state can dispute a speech. It can arrest the person delivering it. It can call a protest leader anti-national, extremist, foreign-funded, politically motivated, whatever the vocabulary of the day demands. But what does it do with a crowd singing? Arrest the chorus?

Nepal Political memory

There is something else happening here, too. Gen-Z's protest music is not necessarily new music. This is where Nepal becomes particularly fascinating.

During Nepal's 2025 Gen-Z protests, Nepathya's Gaun Gaun Bata Utha — originally written and composed by Shyam Tamot during the Panchayat era — returned to the streets. Nepathya shared a clip of the song the day before the protest, and the next morning it was being played through loudspeakers as young protesters gathered at Maitighar Mandala. The song had already carried an anti-establishment history for decades; suddenly, a generation born long after the Panchayat era was singing it again. This is not nostalgia. At least, not only nostalgia. It is political memory.

There is something rather wonderful about the fact that songs survive governments better than governments survive themselves. A political regime can disappear. A leader can fall. A constitutional arrangement can be rewritten. A generation can age. And yet a song can sit quietly somewhere in the cultural memory until somebody needs it again.

Political histories do not survive only in archives, government records or textbooks. They survive orally, through songs and stories, through beaths and daleels, carrying memories of struggle across generations. The song becomes an archive. The archive becomes a voice. And suddenly Gen-Z is not as historically disconnected as we would like to imagine, even if they may have discovered the history on TikTok. The victory stands in their discovery and their willingness to take it forth.

India Wonderfully chaotic

Meanwhile, India has made the whole thing wonderfully chaotic. The Indian protest playlist refuses to respect categories. There is Faiz. There is Hum Dekhenge. There is Sarfaroshi Ki Tamanna. There is Azadi. There is Sadda Haq. There is Rang De Basanti. There is Gully Boy. There is independent rap. There are regional songs. There are old revolutionary songs being rediscovered by people whose parents were not yet born when they were written.

And now there is Michael Jackson. During India's 2026 student protests around the NEET paper-leak controversy, Jackson's "They Don't Care About Us" found a second life through protest edits, Instagram videos and fan communities. A thirty-year-old song could suddenly become the soundtrack to a thirty-second reel of students marching at Jantar Mantar.

It is, objectively, a bizarre playlist.

Faiz and Michael Jackson, from a generation that possibly did not listen to either much while growing up. Revolutionary poetry and pop. Bollywood and independent rap.

An ode to the evergreen Sound of Azaadi

Perhaps the most important thing about Azaadi, however, is that it demonstrates how a sound can escape its original context. The word has a much older political history in Kashmir, and its subsequent movement into Indian student politics and popular culture did not erase that history; it layered new meanings over it. From Kashmir's protests to JNU, from Dub Sharma's Azadi to Gully Boy, the word travelled, was performed by different people and acquired new political lives. Your earlier argument around Azaadi is useful precisely because it resists treating the slogan as something that suddenly appeared in contemporary student politics. This is the peculiar thing about political culture. We like to think that ownership is permanent, and draw binaries. This is our slogan vs their song. This is our history vs their protest. But culture is rather badly behaved.

It refuses ownership. Someone sings your song differently. Someone takes your slogan somewhere you never intended it to go. Someone puts your poem on a placard. Someone makes a meme out of it. And suddenly it belongs to a new generation. Perhaps that is not cultural dilution. Perhaps that is how political memory survives. This is where the idea of a joyful militancy becomes useful. The politics of Shaheen Bagh was striking precisely because it refused the assumption that militancy must reproduce violence and masculinity. Friendship, food, care, laughter and female camaraderie were not distractions from the protest; they were part of how the protest sustained itself. It is also this joyful militancy that penetrated the streets of Jantar Mantar, drawing on people to paint the streets, posterise the walls and open yet another library at a protest site. The friendships and bonds one forms at a protest site are perhaps much more intrinsically aligned, alienating the vices of caste and class, discovering their own sound of dissent.

There is no conclusion to dissent, and therefore none here either.
